# TTC fare demo: research and Papiers curation pack

**Status (17 October 2026):** Agent-curated reading list from original research publishers, author pages, university repositories, and government research. These summaries are **not** human Papiers annotations and none of these papers is claimed to be in our Papiers library yet. The [local JSON index](./data/ttc_fare_evidence.json) and [sample EvidencePacket](./data/ttc_fare_evidence_packet.json) are offline retrieval fixtures. Meet owns this pack; a human team member must read and annotate at least one paper in Papiers for the live sponsor demonstration.

## The actual investigation

**Question:** In a small synthetic Morningside–Highland Creek/UTSC district, how might a *hypothetical* $0.50 increase in the adult TTC pay-as-you-go fare change trips, mode choices, household travel spending, a nearby shop's visits/sales, worker hours/income, and sample fare revenue? Fair Pass and the 47-ride cap remain unchanged. See [demo-data.md](./demo-data.md) for verified Toronto baselines and the precise scenario.

**What the papers can and cannot do:** External studies support the *existence and heterogeneity* of fare response, substitution, accessibility, payment-product choice, and possible local-retail pathways. They do **not** identify a Toronto 2026 fare elasticity, a UTSC shop conversion rate, a store closure probability, TTC-wide revenue, or an emissions effect. Those must be labelled assumptions, simulated outputs, or not estimable. Fares can push some riders toward walking, cycling, driving, another destination, or no trip; **do not assert that a fare rise reduces emissions**. Revenue can rise or fall depending on fares actually paid and lost rides.

### Read first: the five papers that shape the live demo

| Source | What to test in the model | Why it matters / transfer limit |
|---|---|---|
| [Guzman, Gomez & Moncada (2020), Bogotá BRT fare increases](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11116-019-10034-6) | Fare response by income and peak/off-peak purpose. Reported short-run elasticity changes from about −0.565 at one week to −0.408 at one month. | An actual fare-increase panel, but Bogotá BRT in 2001–12 is not Toronto TTC. Use only as an **illustrative sensitivity candidate**, never a calibrated Toronto parameter. |
| [Rosenblum et al. (2019), MIT low-income fare experiment](https://equitytransit.mit.edu/) | Low-income persona may preserve work trips while changing health/social-service/discretionary trips; payment-product choices matter. A 50% discount produced about 30% more trips in this Boston sample. | Randomized evidence for a **discount** in Boston, not a symmetrical estimate for a Toronto increase or proof that shopping visits fall. |
| [UK DfT (2018), bus elasticities and diversion factors](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/bus-elasticities-and-diversion-factors) | Every lost bus trip needs a destination: another mode, different destination, delayed trip, or no trip. | Government evidence synthesis, mostly UK context. Its diversion logic is reusable; its coefficients are not TTC estimates. |
| [Morrissey & Oke (2022), fare-capping/product choice](https://doi.org/10.1177/03611981221082529) | Model fare product and cap eligibility before computing what each rider pays and sample revenue. | Method from another system, not a TTC forecast. The demo's protected Fair Pass and 47-ride cap are explicit scenario rules. |
| [Atherton & Eder (1982), Albany fare-free transit and retail sales](https://trid.trb.org/View/186833) | A possible transit-access → shop-visit → sales path, with effects varying by season, shop type and bus proximity. | Old downtown **fare removal**, not a contemporary fare increase or evidence for a Toronto business closing. Make this path toggleable and assumption-driven. |

## Evidence matrix: 15 sources, ordered by mechanism

The links lead to the original journal/publisher, research team, government publication, or Transportation Research Board record. Where only an abstract/record was checked, the review depth says so. A paper is not a numeric input just because it appears here.

| ID | Original source and checked finding | Demo use | Transfer / review boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| F01 | [Holmgren (2007), *Meta-analysis of public transport demand*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S096585640700047X). Elasticities vary with how service is modelled; the familiar −0.3 figure is conditional. | Separate fare from service, income, car access and fuel costs. | Literature synthesis, not Toronto; publisher abstract checked. |
| F02 | [UK Department for Transport (2018), *Bus elasticities and diversion factors*](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/bus-elasticities-and-diversion-factors). Reviews fare/time response and diversion to other modes or no trip. | Explicit mode/destination/no-trip branches. | UK evidence; report summary checked. |
| F03 | [Guzman, Gomez & Moncada (2020), *Short run fare elasticities for Bogotá's BRT*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11116-019-10034-6). Station panel finds −0.565 at one week, −0.408 after a month; lower-income/off-peak responses are stronger. | Candidate low/base/high short-run response, heterogeneous personas. | BRT, Bogotá, older fares; abstract checked. Never silently apply estimate to TTC. |
| F04 | [Kholodov et al. (2021), Stockholm smartcard natural experiment](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0967070X2100055X). Fare response differs by travel pair and socioeconomic group. | Test different rider groups/distances rather than one elasticity. | Zone-to-flat **fare-structure** reform; not a uniform price rise. Publisher abstract checked. |
| F05 | [Hickey (2005), NYC fare increase, ridership and revenue](https://doi.org/10.3141/1927-27). Riders switched fare products when products rose at different rates. | Revenue requires paid fares and product switching, not posted fare × all boardings. | NYC 2003 fare menu; publisher/TRB bibliographic record and abstract checked. |
| F06 | [Davis (2020/2021), Mexican subway fare changes](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28244). Across three cities, two large hikes and a fare holiday imply elasticities of about −0.23 to −0.32. | Alternative sensitivity anchor; sign and scale cross-check. | Urban **rail**, very large changes and different markets; NBER abstract checked. |
| F07 | [Rosenblum et al. (2019), MIT low-income fare RCT](https://equitytransit.mit.edu/). Boston 50% discount group made about 30% more trips and more health/social-service trips. | Purpose-specific trip decisions; preserve an affordability subgroup and protected fare. | Discount direction, SNAP sample and Boston; research-team report checked. Do not infer a shopping effect. |
| F08 | [Brough, Freedman & Phillips (2025), free transit RCT](https://topcat.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpol.20230460). A temporary free-fare offer had **no statistically significant** effect on paid work hours or earnings; financial/health outcomes were suggestive. | Essential counterevidence: access may change without worker income changing. | Different intervention and sample; journal abstract checked. Worker-hours chain must remain a scenario, not a forecast. |
| F09 | [Nsour (1997), *Characteristics of Shopping Trips by Bus Transit*](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/jpt/vol1/iss3/5/). California survey describes bus-stop distance, transfers, travel time and final walk for shopping trips. | Shop choice should depend on accessibility and purpose, not fare alone. | Descriptive survey; no causal fare-to-sales coefficient; university-hosted abstract checked. |
| F10 | [Atherton & Eder (1982), Albany downtown fare-free transit and retail](https://trid.trb.org/View/186833). Time-series sales-tax panel of 115 retailers suggests positive sales effects for some seasons/types and stores near major routes. | Plausible local spending path, with shop-level variation. | Very old, fare removal, downtown geography; TRB abstract checked. No closure or reverse-effect estimate. |
| F11 | [Studenmund & Connor (1982), *The Free-Fare Transit Experiments*](https://trid.trb.org/View/187374). Off-peak free-fare experiments raised ridership but did not noticeably change commercial and employment activity. | Direct counterweight to F10; include a zero shop effect case. | Old fare-removal evaluations; TRB abstract checked. |
| F12 | [Barri et al. (2022), Toronto/GTHA income, car ownership and transit accessibility](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04556). Transit-use/accessibility relationship varies by income and car access. | Toronto-specific reason to generate heterogeneity in travel constraints. | Accessibility association, **not** fare causal effect; author preprint abstract checked. Do not infer individual joint traits from neighbourhood marginals. |
| F13 | [Morrissey & Oke (2022), fare-capping impact analysis](https://trid.trb.org/View/1930622). Mobile-ticket data feed a fare-product choice model for ridership/revenue/capping. | Explicit cap and payment-product state before revenue. | Different agencies; TRB abstract checked. |
| F14 | [Zheng et al. (2016), *Subways near the subway: Rail transit and neighborhood catering businesses in Beijing*](https://trid.trb.org/View/1423263). New station access is associated with nearby catering openings, diversity and demand. | Retail location/access is a plausible mechanism. | Infrastructure opening, **not** a fare change; Transportation Research Board abstract checked. No numeric transfer. |
| F15 | [Liu & Bardaka (2025), *Exploring the effects of urban rail on the survival of nearby businesses*](https://trid.trb.org/View/2544436). Charlotte LYNX business-survival effects differ by business and proximity. | Warn against a universal business response or immediate closure animation. | Rail investment/long horizon, not fare policy; Transportation Research Board abstract checked. |

## Parameter and claim gate for the MVP

1. **Observed Toronto inputs** come only from the linked source fixture in [demo-data.md](./demo-data.md). A paper cannot turn aggregate census margins into individual rider records.
2. **Fare response:** show at least three illustrative cases, including a zero/low response and a stronger response. F03 and F06 can motivate the breadth but do not define a Toronto confidence interval. Store the chosen value, origin, horizon, subgroup and human decision in the run's evidence ledger.
3. **Mode substitution:** F02 supports the branches, not Toronto branch shares. Let the UI inspect alternative walk/bike/car/no-trip assumptions. Net carbon is **not estimable** without distances, substitution shares and emissions factors.
4. **Household spending and sample revenue:** calculate from actual simulated paid rides, discounts/caps and fares. Label all totals as district sample figures; do not extrapolate to TTC or City budget.
5. **Retail and employment:** F09–F11 disagree or address different interventions. The base case may generate a shop-visit change through agent choices, but its sales conversion/margin and worker-hour rules are explicit synthetic assumptions. Include a zero-effect scenario; never state “a business will close.” F08 blocks an automatic transit-to-earnings claim.
6. **Future view:** show scenario paths under declared assumptions, not statistical forecasts. No GDP, citywide jobs, or NPV without a validated accounting model.

## Papiers human–agent loop to demonstrate

The screenshot shared by James is team guidance for the pitch: show **(1) a completed autonomous investigation, (2) evidence evaluation, (3) an agent-generated next step informed by the result, and (4) a human contribution/understanding**. This sequence also fits the [Papiers sponsor brief](./research.md#papiers-autoresearch--primary).

1. **Human reads and annotates:** Meet or another teammate saves **F03 and F11** in the Papiers app, reads the relevant passage, and writes their *own* note: e.g. “Bogotá BRT fare elasticity is evidence for a sensitivity range, but Albany free-fare experiments found no noticeable commercial effect; keep Toronto shop response uncertain.” This quoted sentence is a **suggestion to write in one's own words**, not an existing human note. Record the Papiers document/note IDs only after they really exist.
2. **Agent investigates:** CLI search/read retrieves the saved passage and actual human note. Research agent proposes a hypothesis and three explicit mechanism assumptions: heterogeneous ride loss, diversion/no trip, and shop effect including zero. Save the search query, IDs, source links and time.
3. **Agent evaluates:** Skeptic compares F03/F06/F07 with F08/F10/F11, grades geography/intervention/population mismatch, and marks the shop/worker link weak. Show evidence-for, evidence-against and gaps in the UI.
4. **Agent proposes next step:** “Run zero, low and high fare response; toggle no shop effect; collect Toronto fare-product and route-specific trip evidence before a real policy recommendation.” This is a proposed experiment, not policy advice.
5. **Human contributes:** A teammate edits/accepts/rejects one proposed assumption in the app, explains why, then reruns. Show before/after agent decisions and metrics with the note and approval linked. An agent-generated note must never masquerade as that human action.

For a network failure, the [JSON index](./data/ttc_fare_evidence.json) and [EvidencePacket fixture](./data/ttc_fare_evidence_packet.json) can replay the **paper evidence**. They cannot be called a live Papiers integration or a human annotation. Add a separate, truthful human note fixture only after a teammate actually authors one and consents to its inclusion.

## Search terms for a teammate curating the Papiers library

Use title/DOI links above in the Papiers **app** to save papers, then annotate there. The documented CLI searches **your existing library**; it does not search all world papers or import new ones. After sign-in, try `papiers search "fare elasticity" --limit 20 --json`, `papiers search "retail transit" --limit 20 --json`, `papiers search "fare capping" --limit 20 --json`, then `papiers read <returned-id> --json`.
